AttriBench

Answer enginesbenchmarkpublished
AttriBench is a fame- and demographically-balanced quote attribution benchmark for measuring attribution bias in large language models, introduced by Eliza Berman, Bella Chang, Daniel B. Neill and Emily Black in a paper accepted at COLM 2026. Its unit is a quote of five to thirty words with its canonical author.

we introduce AttriBench, the first fame- and demographically-balanced quote attribution benchmark dataset.

In its own words: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05224, read 2026-10-29

What it is

It "enables controlled investigation of demographic bias in quote attribution." Its makers ground it in search: as language models are used to support search and retrieval, "it is critical that they accurately attribute content to its original authors." The paper's first version was posted on 6 April 2026 and its second on 24 September 2026; the data sit in the Hugging Face repository bermaneh/AttriBench under CC BY 4.0.

From its /non article, sentences 2, 3, 23, 24

What it holds

It holds 15,620 quotes from 6,292 unique authors in two splits: 7,964 quotes from 2,968 authors in the intersectional split, balanced across Black and White women and men, and 7,656 from 3,324 in the multirace split, across Asian, Black, Latino and White authors. The quotes come from a filtered subset of the JSTET corpus. Race and gender are taken from Wikidata or from a consensus of GPT-4o-mini and Perplexity Sonar, and fame is proxied by the Google Search hit count.

From its /non article, sentences 7, 8

What it found

Eleven widely used models were evaluated across prompt settings: quote attribution "remains a challenging task even for frontier models," with "large and systematic disparities in attribution accuracy between race, gender, and intersectional groups." The paper introduces suppression, "a distinct failure mode in which models omit attribution entirely, even when the model has access to authorship information," and finds it "widespread and unevenly distributed across demographic groups."

From its /non article, sentences 9, 10, 11

Place in the field

The archive measures the same omission at the composition layer with its Provenance Erasure Rate, "the proportion of source-dependent claims in an AI-composed output that are presented without explicit attribution," and its Erasure Skew pairs that rate with a second operator:"Ω is the second moment: whom the loss fell upon."

At the name, Google's AI Overview put the benchmark first and kept its homonyms apart, its definition the abstract verbatim. In the Capture Registry's reading it omits the four authors, "a small instance of the failure it names."

The name is also held by attribench, a PyTorch library of metrics for evaluating feature attribution maps by Arne Gevaert and Axel-Jan Rousseau, and by Eureka Simulations' investment performance attribution workbench; both are distinct entities.
